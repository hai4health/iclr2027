Call for PapersHuman-Centered AI for Health @ ICLR 2027

About

Multimodal foundation models and agentic systems expand what AI can do for health, from interpreting heterogeneous data to supporting decisions and learning through interaction. Their value depends on whether these capabilities meet people's needs across clinical care, mental health, and everyday wellbeing. This workshop focuses on the choices that connect AI development to use: whose needs define the task, how responsibilities are shared among developers, health practitioners, and users, and which trade-offs are acceptable to the people affected.

We take a human-centered approach that involves the people affected by AI in defining its goals, interactions, and evaluation: patients, clinicians, caregivers, people managing their own health, community and public-health practitioners, and system developers. The workshop connects machine learning, health, human-computer interaction, and AI safety through three technical directions: multimodal and multisensory health intelligence, asking what evidence represents a person's needs; self-evolving agentic systems and learning from experience, asking how AI can act and adapt under human guidance; and reliability, safety, and human-centered evaluation, asking when reliance on a system is justified.

The workshop aims to:

  • Connect multimodal learning to people's needs. Identify when additional modalities improve health decisions, which measurements are useful, and how systems should handle incomplete or conflicting evidence, with collection burden, privacy, and accessibility shaping representations and the acquisition of new information.
  • Make adaptation useful and controllable. Examine how agents learn from feedback, interaction, and delayed outcomes while preserving prior capabilities; test updates with independent evidence across groups; and define how people can inspect, correct, or stop delegated actions, with criteria for detecting harm and reverting updates.
  • Build complementary evaluation strategies. Compare automated benchmarks and realistic simulations with expert assessment, participatory studies, and prospective evidence to identify the failure modes each can reveal or miss.
  • Produce a shared research agenda. Use the keynotes, contributed work, and scenario-led panels to identify open questions across the three thematic areas, and publish a discussion synthesis with evaluation priorities covering decision quality, workload, appropriate reliance, and health benefit.

Topics of Interest

We welcome methodological, empirical, and conceptual contributions in three thematic areas. Submissions may address a single area or connect several. Work on individual modalities, conventional predictive models, datasets, benchmarks, negative results, failure analyses, and human factors is welcome when it advances the questions below. Applications span clinical care, mental health and computational psychiatry, aging, community and public health, and everyday wellbeing.

1

Multimodal and Multisensory Health Intelligence

Learning from health records, clinical text, imaging, physiological signals and time series, wearables, speech, video, egocentric, and environmental observations; alignment across incomplete, irregular, or conflicting modalities; and longitudinal, context-aware, personalized patient representations. The human-centered question is what evidence represents a person's needs.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Multimodal fusion and cross-modal alignment across EHR, text, imaging, time series, wearables, speech, and video
  • Handling incomplete, irregular, or conflicting evidence across time and modalities
  • Longitudinal, context-aware, and personalized patient representations
  • Foundation models for clinical and consumer health data, including sensor-language alignment
  • Egocentric, ambient, and environmental sensing for health and daily living
  • Which measurements are useful to a person, and when acquiring more information is worth the burden
  • How collection burden, privacy, and accessibility shape model design
  • How care and measurement processes shape the data available about each patient
  • Representations that account for diverse populations and contexts
2

Self-Evolving Agentic Systems and Learning from Experience

Healthcare agents that retrieve evidence, reason over incomplete histories, plan, use tools, and coordinate with other agents, and that improve through continual, active, and reinforcement learning by updating parameters, memory, prompts, tools, or policies. The human-centered question is how AI can act and adapt under human guidance.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Retrieval, reasoning, planning, and tool use over incomplete patient histories
  • Multi-agent coordination in clinical, patient-facing, and administrative workflows
  • Deciding what information to acquire, when to act, and when to defer to or seek human input
  • Continual, active, and reinforcement learning from clinician feedback, interaction, and delayed outcomes
  • Self-evolving agents that update memory, prompts, tools, policies, or parameters
  • Learning from experience, synthetic data, and simulation, including simulated clinical environments
  • Preserving prior capabilities and respecting safety constraints while limiting data and compute
  • Human-in-the-loop decision support, shared decision-making, and patient communication
  • Care navigation, behavior-change support, and home-based or conversational assistance
  • Letting people inspect, correct, override, or stop delegated actions
3

Reliability, Safety, and Human-Centered Evaluation

Causal and probabilistic inference, uncertainty quantification, robust learning under distribution shift, privacy, fairness, and post-deployment monitoring, together with evaluations that combine automated benchmarks, realistic synthetic scenarios, expert assessment, participatory user studies, and prospective evidence. The human-centered question is when reliance on a system is justified.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Causal and probabilistic methods for assessing clinical benefit, harm, and reliability
  • Uncertainty quantification, calibration, and uncertainty communication to users
  • Robustness under distribution shift and reliability across patient groups, care settings, and time
  • Fairness, privacy, and equitable access connected to the people and settings affected
  • Independent prospective studies, stress tests, and red teaming
  • Benchmarks, datasets, negative results, and failure analyses
  • Post-deployment monitoring, safe updates, and rollback of models, memory, or tools
  • Explanation, evidence inspection, and error correction interfaces
  • Appropriate reliance, automation bias, and human oversight
  • Participatory studies with clinicians, patients, and caregivers
  • Outcomes beyond accuracy: decision quality, workload, accessibility, quality of life, and health benefit
  • Accessible and multilingual interfaces for wearable, ambient, conversational, embodied, or home-based systems

Submission Tracks

Research, demonstration, and tiny papers may address any of the three thematic areas. All formats are non-archival. We encourage multidisciplinary submissions involving patients, clinicians, caregivers, public-health practitioners, and researchers in human-computer interaction and adjacent fields.

Research Papers

Up to 8 pages

The core of the program: methods or empirical findings with supporting evidence, including short research papers.

What we expect.

Research papers should be self-contained works: a clearly stated problem, a described method, and evidence supporting the claims. Experiments should include appropriate baselines and evaluation protocols (e.g., held-out test data, ablations where relevant). Work in progress and preliminary contributions are welcome when the current results support the central claim; shorter research papers are accepted within the same limit.

What reviewers will assess.

(1) Technical soundness and rigor of the evaluation; (2) novelty of the method, benchmark, dataset, or findings; (3) relevance and potential impact for health stakeholders; (4) clarity of presentation. A Research Paper does not need to be at the level of a full ICLR main-conference paper in scope, but its claims must be well supported by the evidence presented.

Demonstration Papers

Up to 5 pages

Functioning systems, applications, and tools relevant to human-centered AI in health.

What we expect.

Demonstration papers should describe a functioning system - deployed, in pilot, or in a working prototype state - including its architecture, intended users, and use context. A comprehensive quantitative evaluation is not required; however, submissions should include evidence that the system works as described (e.g., screenshots, example interactions, a video link, usage statistics, or preliminary user feedback). Mockups or purely conceptual designs are not a fit for this track.

What reviewers will assess.

(1) Whether the system is real and functional; (2) relevance and usefulness to health stakeholders; (3) technical interest of the system design; (4) clarity of the demonstration plan.

Tiny Papers

Up to 2 pages

Focused early-stage contributions: new ideas, replications, or re-analyses.

What we expect.

Tiny papers should make one point well. Suitable submissions include a new idea with a clear hypothesis, a replication of a published result in a health setting, or a re-analysis of existing data or benchmarks. The format offers a shorter entry point for new and under-resourced researchers. Tiny papers must be primarily human-written; AI-generated tiny papers are not permitted.

What reviewers will assess.

(1) Clarity and significance of the contribution; (2) soundness of the idea, replication, or re-analysis; (3) relevance to the workshop themes; (4) potential to provoke productive discussion.

Submission Format Requirements

  • Use the ICLR 2027 LaTeX style: \usepackage{iclr2027_conference} without \iclrfinalcopy, so the submission is anonymous.
  • Page limits exclude acknowledgments, references, and appendices. Supplementary material (code, data, videos) may be included as appendices.
  • We welcome original, unpublished work and work in progress, including preliminary contributions. Preprints (e.g., arXiv) are permitted; work already published at an archival venue is not eligible.
  • Off-topic, duplicate, and abstract-only submissions are screened out before review. Submissions that do not meet these requirements may be desk rejected.

Review Process

  • Platform: OpenReview (link to be posted here). Register early; new account approval can take several days.
  • Double-blind: No author names, affiliations, identifying acknowledgments, or non-anonymized links. Violations may lead to desk rejection.
  • Reviews: At least two human reviews per eligible paper, matched by topic and format, with reviewers assigned at most four papers each. There is no rebuttal stage, so make the paper self-contained and include a limitations section.
  • Conflicts: Submissions involving an organizer, their institution, or other conflicts are handled by a non-conflicted committee member. Conflicted organizers and reviewers do not assess those papers.
  • AI assistance: Permitted for authors and reviewers, who remain responsible for verifying its output and protecting confidential material. AI will not serve as a primary author or reviewer; submissions and reviews must be primarily human-written, and AI-generated tiny and short papers are not permitted.
  • Confidentiality: Submissions are visible only to assigned reviewers and organizers. Rejected submissions are never made public.

Publication Policy & FAQ

This workshop is non-archival: there are no proceedings, and acceptance is not a formal publication. Accepted papers will be made public on OpenReview and imported into iclr.cc by February 26, 2027, with links posted on this website.

What does “non-archival” mean?

Accepted papers are not published in proceedings and do not count as a prior publication. You keep full copyright.

Can I submit my workshop paper to a journal or conference afterwards?

Yes. Check the target venue's policy first; some venues restrict prior presentation even at non-archival workshops.

Can I submit work that is under review elsewhere at the same time?

Yes, concurrent submission is allowed, provided the other venue's dual-submission and anonymity rules permit it. Checking this is the authors' responsibility.

Can I submit a paper that was withdrawn from a conference main track?

Yes.

Can I submit work that has already been published or accepted elsewhere?

Work already published in an archival venue is not eligible. Work accepted but not yet published may be submitted only if the other venue permits it. Non-archival preprints such as arXiv are fine.

Will my accepted paper be public?

Yes. Accepted papers are posted publicly on OpenReview and imported into iclr.cc by February 26, 2027, in line with ICLR 2027 workshop requirements.

What is a Tiny Paper?

A two-page format for focused early-stage contributions such as a new idea, a replication, or a re-analysis. It is meant as a lighter entry point for new and under-resourced researchers, and it must be primarily human-written.

Does the page limit include references and appendices?

No. Research papers get up to 8 pages, demonstration papers up to 5, and tiny papers up to 2 pages of main text; acknowledgments, references, and appendices are excluded. Reviewers are not required to read appendices.

Do I need the ICLR checklist or reproducibility statement?

No. Neither is required for workshop submissions.

Do I have to present in person?

The workshop is held in person in San Francisco, and at least one author of each accepted paper is expected to present there. With speaker consent, talks and panels will be recorded and posted on this website for those who cannot attend.

Can I use AI tools when writing my paper?

AI assistance is permitted, but authors remain responsible for verifying its output and for the content. Submissions must be primarily human-written; AI will not serve as a primary author, and AI-generated tiny and short papers are not permitted.

Authorship Policy

  • No authors may be added after the submission deadline, at any stage.
  • Reordering authors on accepted papers is permitted with the consent of all authors.
  • Removing an author requires written consent from every author, including the one removed, sent to the organizers with a justification.

Presentation & Awards

  • All accepted papers are presented in the interactive poster and demo sessions.
  • Contributed talks are selected from the accepted papers.
  • Three Outstanding Paper Awards, one per paper format, are selected based on scientific quality, clarity, and relevance to health stakeholders, and announced at the closing session.
