About

Healthcare generates a growing body of electronic health records, images, physiological measurements, and patient-reported information that could support more timely and individualized care. Medical vision models, large language models, and tool-using agents can help interpret these data across prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring. Yet strong model performance alone does not establish effective human-AI collaboration: models must learn from incomplete data, remain reliable across populations and care settings, communicate uncertainty, and offer routes for correction. For systems that learn from feedback, these questions recur after every update.

Human-centered AI starts from the goals, capabilities, and constraints of clinicians, patients, and caregivers, and involves them in defining tasks, designing interactions, and evaluating results. This workshop brings together the machine learning, healthcare, and human-computer interaction communities to ask how healthcare AI can become more capable while remaining useful and accountable to its users, through research on multimodal learning, agentic reasoning, and systems that improve through experience.

The workshop aims to:

  • Connect multimodal learning to clinical decisions. Identify when additional modalities improve a decision, how systems should handle incomplete or conflicting evidence, and when acquiring more information is worth the burden on a patient or caregiver.
  • Support reliable decisions across patients and care settings. Clarify when clinicians and patients can rely on AI, when human review is needed, and what evidence shows that using a system improves care.
  • Make learning from feedback and experience testable. Use independent evidence to test whether healthcare agents improve through clinician feedback, interaction, and delayed outcomes, and define criteria for detecting harm and reverting updates.
  • Define meaningful human oversight and collaboration. Study uncertainty communication, evidence inspection, correction, and deferral, and evaluate effects on workload, autonomy, trust, and outcomes.
  • Build shared evaluation practices and collaborations. Bring together causal inference, probabilistic modeling, machine learning, AI safety, clinical research, and human-centered AI to develop shared evaluation practices.

Topics of Interest

We welcome methodological, empirical, and conceptual contributions addressing AI for healthcare and medicine. Work on individual modalities, conventional predictive models, datasets, negative results, or human factors is welcome when it advances the broader questions above.

1

Multimodal and Multisensory Representations for Patient Understanding

Methods that learn personalized patient representations from health records, clinical text, imaging, physiological time series, wearables, speech, video, and other patient- or environment-derived sensory signals, including how to align and combine incomplete, irregular, or conflicting evidence across time and modalities.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Multimodal fusion across EHR, text, imaging, time series, and wearables
  • Aligning incomplete, irregular, or conflicting evidence over time
  • Speech, video, and ambient sensing for patient understanding
  • Modeling how care and measurement processes shape available data
  • Personalized patient representations and longitudinal modeling
2

Agentic Reasoning and Clinical Workflows

Healthcare agents that retrieve evidence, reason over incomplete patient histories, plan actions, use tools, and coordinate with other agents. A central question is how these systems should decide what information to acquire, when to act, and when to defer to or seek human input within clinical, patient-facing, or administrative workflows.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Tool-using and multi-agent systems for diagnosis, triage, and care coordination
  • Evidence retrieval and reasoning over incomplete patient histories
  • Deciding what information to acquire and when to act or defer
  • Agents in clinical documentation, administrative, and patient-facing workflows
  • Simulated environments and benchmarks for interactive clinical agents
3

AI Safety, Evaluation, and Deployment

Causal and probabilistic methods, independent prospective studies, and stress tests to assess clinical benefit, harm, and reliability under distribution shift and across patient populations, along with datasets, benchmarks, negative results, and failure analyses that inform monitoring, safe updates, or rollback.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Causal and probabilistic evaluation of clinical benefit and harm
  • Reliability under distribution shift and across patient subgroups
  • Prospective studies, stress tests, and red teaming
  • Datasets, benchmarks, negative results, and failure analyses
  • Monitoring, safe model updates, and rollback
4

Learning from Experience and Self-Evolution

How healthcare agents adapt their memory, prompts, tools, policies, or parameters using human feedback, delayed outcomes, and changing patient needs, including continual, active, and reinforcement learning approaches that use experience, synthetic data, or simulation while preserving prior capabilities and respecting safety constraints.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Continual, active, and reinforcement learning from clinical feedback
  • Learning from delayed outcomes and changing patient needs
  • Adapting memory, prompts, tools, policies, or parameters safely
  • Synthetic data and simulation for experience-driven learning
  • Preserving prior capabilities while limiting data and compute requirements
5

Human-AI Collaboration and Inclusive Care

Participatory research with clinicians, patients, and caregivers on systems that explain their outputs, communicate uncertainty, and allow people to inspect evidence, correct errors, or override decisions, including appropriate reliance, shared decision-making, and accessible, multilingual interfaces that respect patient preferences.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
  • Uncertainty communication, evidence inspection, correction, and override
  • Appropriate reliance, automation bias, and shared decision-making
  • Participatory design with clinicians, patients, and caregivers
  • Accessible and multilingual interfaces for wearable, ambient, conversational, embodied, or home-based systems
  • Workload, autonomy, trust, equity, and patient preferences as evaluation goals

Submission Tracks

Research, demonstration, and position papers may address any of the five topic areas. We encourage multidisciplinary submissions involving stakeholders from healthcare, public policy, and adjacent fields.

Research Papers

Up to 9 pages

The core of the program: methodological advances and empirical studies in human-centered AI for health.

What we expect.

Research papers should be complete, self-contained works: a clearly stated problem, a described method, and empirical evidence supporting the claims. Experiments should include appropriate baselines and evaluation protocols (e.g., held-out test data, ablations where relevant). Work in progress is acceptable only if the current results already substantiate the central claim - promising directions without supporting evidence are better suited as short position papers.

What reviewers will assess.

(1) Technical soundness and rigor of the evaluation; (2) novelty of the method, benchmark, dataset, or findings; (3) relevance and potential impact for health; (4) clarity of presentation. A Research Paper does not need to be at the level of a full ICLR main-conference paper in scope, but its claims must be well supported by the evidence presented.

Demonstration Papers

Up to 5 pages

Working systems, applications, and tools relevant to human-centered AI in health.

What we expect.

Demonstration papers should describe a functioning system - deployed, in pilot, or in a working prototype state - including its architecture, intended users, and use context. A comprehensive quantitative evaluation is not required; however, submissions should include evidence that the system works as described (e.g., screenshots, example interactions, a video link, usage statistics, or preliminary user feedback). Mockups or purely conceptual designs are not a fit for this track.

What reviewers will assess.

(1) Whether the system is real and functional; (2) relevance and usefulness to health stakeholders; (3) technical interest of the system design; (4) clarity of the demonstration plan.

Position Papers

Up to 5 pages

Perspectives, analyses, and proposals on policy, governance, evaluation practices, or deployment strategies for human-centered AI in health.

What we expect.

Position papers should advance a clear, well-argued thesis. New experiments are not required, but arguments must be grounded in evidence - literature, regulatory precedent, clinical experience, or documented deployments. A strong position paper engages seriously with counterarguments rather than only advocating one side.

What reviewers will assess.

(1) Clarity and significance of the position; (2) quality and grounding of the argument; (3) potential to provoke productive discussion in the community; (4) multidisciplinary relevance.

Submission Format Requirements

  • Use the ICLR 2027 LaTeX style: \usepackage{iclr2027_conference} without \iclrfinalcopy, so the submission is anonymous.
  • Page limits exclude references and appendices. Supplementary material (code, data, videos) may be included as appendices.
  • Submissions must be original and unpublished. Preprints (e.g., arXiv) are fine; work already published at an archival venue is not eligible.
  • Submissions that do not meet these requirements may be desk rejected.

Review Process

  • Platform: OpenReview (link to be posted here). Register early; new account approval can take several days.
  • Double-blind: No author names, affiliations, identifying acknowledgments, or non-anonymized links. Violations may lead to desk rejection.
  • Reviews: At least two program committee reviews per submission, matched by topic and track. There is no rebuttal stage, so make the paper self-contained and include a limitations section.
  • Conflicts: Submissions involving an organizer or an organizer's institution are handled by a non-conflicted committee member.
  • Confidentiality: Submissions are visible only to assigned reviewers and organizers. Rejected submissions are never made public.

Publication Policy & FAQ

This workshop is non-archival: there are no proceedings, and acceptance is not a formal publication. Accepted papers are posted publicly on OpenReview by default; authors may opt out before the camera-ready deadline.

What does “non-archival” mean?

Accepted papers are not published in proceedings and do not count as a prior publication. You keep full copyright.

Can I submit my workshop paper to a journal or conference afterwards?

Yes. Check the target venue's policy first; some venues restrict prior presentation even at non-archival workshops.

Can I submit work that is under review elsewhere at the same time?

Yes, concurrent submission is allowed, provided the other venue's dual-submission and anonymity rules permit it. Checking this is the authors' responsibility.

Can I submit a paper that was withdrawn from a conference main track?

Yes.

Can I submit work that has already been published or accepted elsewhere?

Work already published in an archival venue is not eligible. Work accepted but not yet published may be submitted only if the other venue permits it. Non-archival preprints such as arXiv are fine.

Will my accepted paper be public?

By default, yes: accepted papers are posted on OpenReview after the camera-ready stage. Authors may opt out by contacting the organizers before the camera-ready deadline, in which case the PDF is not posted. A reminder is sent before that deadline.

Does the page limit include references and appendices?

No. Research papers get up to 9 pages and demonstration and position papers up to 5 pages of main text; acknowledgments, references, and appendices are excluded. Reviewers are not required to read appendices.

Do I need the ICLR checklist or reproducibility statement?

No. Neither is required for workshop submissions.

Do I have to present in person?

At least one author of each accepted paper is expected to present in person at the workshop in San Francisco.

Can I use AI tools when writing my paper?

AI tools may assist with writing and editing, but authors remain fully responsible for the content. Papers generated primarily by AI systems are not permitted, consistent with ICLR 2027 policy.

Authorship Policy

  • No authors may be added after the submission deadline, at any stage.
  • Reordering authors on accepted papers is permitted with the consent of all authors.
  • Removing an author requires written consent from every author, including the one removed, sent to the organizers with a justification.

Presentation & Awards

  • All accepted papers are presented as posters.
  • Spotlight talks are selected from the accepted papers.
  • Three Outstanding Paper Awards are selected, one for each paper track, and announced at the closing session.
