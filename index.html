About

Multimodal foundation models and agentic systems expand what AI can do for health, from interpreting heterogeneous data to supporting decisions and learning through interaction. Their value depends on whether these capabilities meet people's needs across clinical care, mental health, and everyday wellbeing.

This workshop focuses on the choices that connect AI development to use: whose needs define the task, how responsibilities are shared among developers, health practitioners, and users, and which trade-offs are acceptable to the people affected. We bring together machine learning, health, human-computer interaction, and AI safety around the three directions below.

The workshop aims to

  1. Connect multimodal learning to people's needs

    which modalities and measurements improve health decisions, and how collection burden, privacy, and accessibility shape them.

  2. Make adaptation useful and controllable

    how agents learn from feedback and delayed outcomes while people can inspect, correct, or stop them, and harmful updates can be reverted.

  3. Build complementary evaluation strategies

    what benchmarks, simulations, expert assessment, participatory studies, and prospective evidence each reveal or miss.

  4. Produce a shared research agenda

    a public synthesis of open questions and evaluation priorities covering decision quality, workload, appropriate reliance, and health benefit.

Topics of Interest

We welcome methodological, empirical, and conceptual contributions in three thematic areas. Submissions may address a single area or connect several.

Work on individual modalities, conventional predictive models, datasets, benchmarks, negative results, failure analyses, and human factors is welcome when it advances the questions below. Applications span clinical care, mental health and computational psychiatry, aging, community and public health, and everyday wellbeing.

1

Multimodal and Multisensory Health Intelligence

Learning from health records, clinical text, imaging, physiological signals and time series, wearables, speech, video, egocentric, and environmental observations; alignment across incomplete, irregular, or conflicting modalities; and longitudinal, context-aware, personalized patient representations. The human-centered question is what evidence represents a person's needs.

  • Multimodal fusion and cross-modal alignment across EHR, text, imaging, time series, wearables, speech, and video
  • Handling incomplete, irregular, or conflicting evidence across time and modalities
  • Longitudinal, context-aware, and personalized patient representations
  • Foundation models for clinical and consumer health data, including sensor-language alignment
  • Egocentric, ambient, and environmental sensing for health and daily living
  • Which measurements are useful to a person, and when acquiring more information is worth the burden
  • How collection burden, privacy, and accessibility shape model design
  • How care and measurement processes shape the data available about each patient
  • Representations that account for diverse populations and contexts
2

Self-Evolving Agentic Systems and Learning from Experience

Healthcare agents that retrieve evidence, reason over incomplete histories, plan, use tools, and coordinate with other agents, and that improve through continual, active, and reinforcement learning by updating parameters, memory, prompts, tools, or policies. The human-centered question is how AI can act and adapt under human guidance.

  • Retrieval, reasoning, planning, and tool use over incomplete patient histories
  • Multi-agent coordination in clinical, patient-facing, and administrative workflows
  • Deciding what information to acquire, when to act, and when to defer to or seek human input
  • Continual, active, and reinforcement learning from clinician feedback, interaction, and delayed outcomes
  • Self-evolving agents that update memory, prompts, tools, policies, or parameters
  • Learning from experience, synthetic data, and simulation, including simulated clinical environments
  • Preserving prior capabilities and respecting safety constraints while limiting data and compute
  • Human-in-the-loop decision support, shared decision-making, and patient communication
  • Care navigation, behavior-change support, and home-based or conversational assistance
  • Letting people inspect, correct, override, or stop delegated actions
3

Reliability, Safety, and Human-Centered Evaluation

Causal and probabilistic inference, uncertainty quantification, robust learning under distribution shift, privacy, fairness, and post-deployment monitoring, together with evaluations that combine automated benchmarks, realistic synthetic scenarios, expert assessment, participatory user studies, and prospective evidence. The human-centered question is when reliance on a system is justified.

  • Causal and probabilistic methods for assessing clinical benefit, harm, and reliability
  • Uncertainty quantification, calibration, and uncertainty communication to users
  • Robustness under distribution shift and reliability across patient groups, care settings, and time
  • Fairness, privacy, and equitable access connected to the people and settings affected
  • Independent prospective studies, stress tests, and red teaming
  • Benchmarks, datasets, negative results, and failure analyses
  • Post-deployment monitoring, safe updates, and rollback of models, memory, or tools
  • Explanation, evidence inspection, and error correction interfaces
  • Appropriate reliance, automation bias, and human oversight
  • Participatory studies with clinicians, patients, and caregivers
  • Outcomes beyond accuracy: decision quality, workload, accessibility, quality of life, and health benefit
  • Accessible and multilingual interfaces for wearable, ambient, conversational, embodied, or home-based systems

Submission Formats

Three non-archival formats across all thematic areas. Limits exclude acknowledgments, references, and appendices.

Research Papers

Up to 8 pages

Methods or empirical findings with supporting evidence, including short research papers. A clearly stated problem, a described method, appropriate baselines, and evaluation that supports the claims. Work in progress is welcome when current results support the central claim.

Demonstration and Position Papers

Up to 5 pages

Either a functioning system, deployed, in pilot, or in working prototype state, with its intended users and evidence that it works as described (screenshots, interactions, a video, usage data, or user feedback); or a clear position on health AI research or practice, supported by evidence and engaging seriously with counterarguments.

Tiny Papers

Up to 2 pages

Focused early-stage contributions that make one point well: a new idea with a clear hypothesis, a replication in a health setting, or a re-analysis of existing data or benchmarks. A lighter entry point for new and under-resourced researchers; must be primarily human-written.

Review criteria

Soundness

rigor of the method, system, or argument and its evidence.

Novelty

of the method, system, benchmark, dataset, idea, or findings.

Relevance and impact

for health stakeholders and the workshop themes.

Clarity

of presentation, including a discussion of limitations.

All accepted papers are presented in the interactive poster and demo sessions; contributed talks are selected from them. Three Outstanding Paper Awards, one per format, are announced at the closing session.

How to Submit

Format

ICLR 2027 LaTeX style with \usepackage{iclr2027_conference} and without \iclrfinalcopy. Supplementary material (code, data, videos) goes in appendices.

Anonymity

double-blind. No author names, affiliations, identifying acknowledgments, or non-anonymized links; violations may lead to desk rejection.

Eligibility

original, unpublished work and work in progress. Preprints such as arXiv are permitted; work already published at an archival venue is not. Off-topic, duplicate, and abstract-only submissions are screened out.

Platform

OpenReview (link to be posted here). Register early; new account approval can take several days.

Reviews

at least two human reviews per paper, matched by topic and format, with at most four papers per reviewer. No rebuttal stage, so make the paper self-contained.

Conflicts

submissions involving an organizer or their institution are handled by a non-conflicted committee member.

AI assistance

permitted for authors and reviewers, who remain responsible for verifying its output and protecting confidential material. Submissions and reviews must be primarily human-written; AI-generated tiny and short papers are not permitted.

Publication Policy & FAQ

  • Non-archival. No proceedings or formal publication.
  • Authors keep copyright. Submit elsewhere afterwards.
  • Public on OpenReview by February 26, 2027, with links posted here.
Can I submit work that is under review elsewhere, was withdrawn from a main track, or send my paper elsewhere afterwards?

Yes to all three, because the workshop is non-archival. Concurrent submission requires that the other venue's dual-submission and anonymity rules permit it, and some venues restrict prior presentation even at non-archival workshops; checking this is the authors' responsibility.

Can I submit work that has already been published or accepted elsewhere?

Work already published in an archival venue is not eligible. Work accepted but not yet published may be submitted only if the other venue permits it. Non-archival preprints are fine.

Will my accepted paper be public?

Yes. Accepted papers are posted publicly on OpenReview and imported into iclr.cc by February 26, 2027, in line with ICLR 2027 workshop requirements.

What is a Tiny Paper, and do page limits include references?

A two-page format for focused early-stage contributions such as a new idea, a replication, or a re-analysis. For all formats, acknowledgments, references, and appendices are excluded from the limit, and reviewers are not required to read appendices. No ICLR checklist or reproducibility statement is required.

Do I have to present in person?

The workshop is held in person in San Francisco, and at least one author of each accepted paper is expected to present there. With speaker consent, talks and panels will be recorded and posted on this website for those who cannot attend.

Can I use AI tools when writing my paper?

AI assistance is permitted, but authors remain responsible for verifying its output and for the content. Submissions must be primarily human-written; AI will not serve as a primary author, and AI-generated tiny and short papers are not permitted.

Can the author list change after submission?

No authors may be added after the deadline at any stage. Reordering is permitted for accepted papers with every author's consent. Removing an author requires written consent from every author, including the one removed, sent to the organizers with a justification.
